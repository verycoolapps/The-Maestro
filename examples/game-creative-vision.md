---

> **Context:** An independent Indonesian game studio is developing a narrative adventure game for PC and console. They have a world concept — a fantastical Indonesian archipelago where the boundaries between the physical world and the spirit world are permeable — but need creative direction to transform the concept into a coherent, specific, emotionally powerful creative vision. They want the game to feel distinctly Indonesian without being an "Indonesian heritage game" in the same way a museum exhibit is "about Indonesia."

## THE CREATIVE VISION STATEMENT

RAGAM JIWA is a game about the specific loneliness of people who can see things others cannot — and the specific community that forms between them.

In the Nusantara of this game, the boundary between the living world and the spirit world is not a wall but a membrane — thin, permeable, different for different people. Some people are born with the capacity to perceive this membrane more clearly than others. The Javanese call these people *wong cilik sakti* — ordinary people with extraordinary perception.

This capacity is not a superpower in the sense games have trained us to understand superpowers. It is not a weapon or an advantage. It is a responsibility that most people didn't ask for and cannot put down. It makes you different from your family. It makes conversations difficult. It makes the world richer and heavier simultaneously.

RAGAM JIWA is set in a fantastical archipelago inspired by the specific textures of Indonesian cultural and mythological reality — not the romanticized tourist poster version, but the actual, complicated, contradictory, extraordinarily rich reality of a civilization that is simultaneously ancient and rapidly modernizing, deeply spiritual and deeply practical, individual and profoundly communal.

The game's question, the one it will never fully answer: **Is it possible to fully belong to a world you see more completely than the people around you?**

**How the player should feel at the end:**
Not saved. Not triumphant. The feeling of a conversation with someone who truly understood you — that specific warmth and melancholy that comes from being known, combined with the knowledge that the conversation will end.

---

## CORE EMOTIONAL PROMISE

The specific feeling that RAGAM JIWA is designed to reliably deliver:

**"The ache and beauty of seeing the world in a way others don't — and finding the people who make that bearable."**

This is not a game about being special. It is a game about the specific cost and gift of perception — and about the community of people who pay that cost together.

Every scene, every mechanic, every piece of dialogue is designed to serve this emotional promise. When a design decision moves away from this feeling, it must be reconsidered.

## WORLDBUILDING BIBLE — EXCERPT

### THE WORLD: NUSANTARA RATU

**Geographic Character:**
The world of RAGAM JIWA is an archipelago of roughly 60 islands, ranging from the size of a small city to vast landmasses. The world draws its geography from the Indonesian archipelago but is not a recreation of it — the islands have been reorganized into a configuration that serves the game's narrative needs.

The primary islands:
- **Tanah Utama** (The Main Land): The commercial and political center. Dense, urban, modernizing. The specific energy of a Southeast Asian city in rapid transition — older neighborhoods being absorbed by towers, street food vendors operating next to glass-fronted cafes, the sound of motorcycles and gamelan from two open windows simultaneously.
- **Nusa Barat** (Western Isle): The island of learning and religion. Home to the oldest universities, the most ancient temples, and the most contested questions about what Indonesian spiritual tradition means in a contemporary world.
- **Kepulauan Timur** (Eastern Islands): A chain of smaller islands where the membrane between living and spirit worlds is thinnest. Many inhabitants of these islands are *wong cilik sakti* without knowing there's a word for it. The old ways are practiced not as heritage but as function.
- **Pulau Tengah** (The Middle Island): A floating island that moves slowly between the others. Its position in the story changes based on player actions. Home to the Rumah Ragam — the House of Seeing, the community at the center of the game's narrative.

**The Membrane:**
In RAGAM JIWA, the spirit world is not "above" or "below" the physical world — it is adjacent. The membrane between them has texture, permeability, and history. In places where many people have lived and died, the membrane is thin. In places that have been sacred for centuries, it has specific characteristics — not all thinnings are the same.

Mechanically: the player can perceive the membrane's qualities through the protagonist's specific sensitivity. This manifests visually as a slight shift in color temperature, a specific visual frequency that is unique to each player encounter with the spirit-adjacent world. The game's visual filter for spirit-world proximity is a design decision that must feel consistent, legible, and beautiful — never merely "glitchy" or "supernatural."

---

**Cultural Foundation — THE FOUR PRINCIPLES (Empat Prinsip):**

The world of RAGAM JIWA organizes spiritual experience around four principles drawn from Indonesian philosophical traditions:

1. **Rupa** (Form) — The physical world as it appears to ordinary perception. What can be seen, touched, measured.

2. **Jiwa** (Soul / Spirit) — The animating force within living and formerly-living things. In this world, Jiwa does not simply depart at death — it transforms, moves, and sometimes requires resolution before it can fully transition.

3. **Rasa** (Feeling / Sensation) — The capacity for perception that includes but exceeds the five senses. *Wong cilik sakti* have heightened Rasa — they can perceive what others cannot.

4. **Karsa** (Will / Agency) — The capacity to act with intention. The question the game poses: when you can see what others cannot, what is your responsibility to act?

---

**Mythological Framework:**

The world draws from Indonesian mythology without directly replicating any single tradition. The key mythological forces in the game:

**The Hyang:** Ancient spirit presences associated with specific places — mountains, rivers, ancient trees, sacred sites. They are not gods in the Western sense — they are awareness that has accumulated in specific locations over millennia. They are neither benevolent nor malevolent — they are present, with their own nature.

**The Lelembut:** Spirits of the recently deceased who have not yet fully transitioned. Many are simply confused. Some are attached to something unresolved. The game's central activities often involve helping Lelembut find resolution — not through battle, but through understanding what they need.

**The Raksasa Waktu (Time Giants):** Ancient beings that exist at the edges of the world's history. The player encounters them as fragments — geological formations, ancient structures, the feeling of impossible age. They are not antagonists. They are context.

**The Pemimpin Roh (Spirit Leaders):** The game's closest thing to antagonists — human practitioners who have learned to control rather than perceive the spirit world. Their power corrupts their perception, and they mistake control for understanding.

---

### FACTIONS AND COMMUNITIES

**The Rumah Ragam (House of Seeing):**
The community the player joins. Not an organization or a school — a household, in the extended Indonesian sense: a collection of people who have found each other because they needed each other. Located on Pulau Tengah (the middle island).

This is the community the game is fundamentally about. Its members range from an elderly grandmother who has been managing her *Rasa* for 70 years to a teenage boy who just discovered his sensitivity three months ago. They are not unified by ideology but by the shared experience of seeing more than most people see.

The household's social dynamics are as complex as any family: longstanding arguments, hidden affections, collaborative tensions, the specific hierarchy of knowledge and experience, the specific equality of need.

**The Paguyuban Formal (Formal Associations):**
The bureaucratic structures that have been created around spiritual practice in this world — universities, governmental departments, religious organizations that have an institutional stake in controlling how the spirit world is understood and managed. Some members are sincerely helpful. Others are corrupt. Most are somewhere between: people trying to do good things within systems that limit goodness.

**The Dunia Biasa (Ordinary World):**
The 85% of the population who don't have heightened *Rasa* and have no direct experience of the spirit world. They are not ignorant — many have sophisticated cultural frameworks for understanding the spirit world secondhand. But their experience is different, and that difference matters. The game treats them with the same complexity as the *wong cilik sakti*: they are not simply "people who don't understand." They are people whose experience is different.

## PLAYER EXPERIENCE ARC

### Opening (First 45 Minutes)
The player arrives in Tanah Utama — the main island's urban center. They are SARI, 24, recently arrived from a small town in Nusa Barat. Sari has spent her life managing the perception she can't quite explain and can't quite turn off. She is not traumatized by it — she has simply learned to manage it, the way some people manage chronic pain.

The opening sequence is ordinary: Sari navigating her first week in a new city. Finding her room. Finding her job. The first encounters with the city's spiritual texture are small and easily manageable. The player learns to perceive the membrane through small interactions — not through explicit tutorial but through the natural exploration of a new environment.

The first chapter establishes the game's emotional register: not the adrenaline of a new power, but the specific quiet of a person who has carried something alone for a long time, managing in a new place.

### Discovery (Hours 2–8)
Sari encounters the Rumah Ragam through a Lelembut situation she can't manage alone — the first time her individual *Rasa* is insufficient for what she encounters. The community finds her, in the way that communities find the people who need them when those people are ready.

This section of the game is about the specific joy and discomfort of being known. The Rumah Ragam's members see Sari in ways she hasn't been seen before — which is simultaneously the thing she has always wanted and the most terrifying experience of her life.

### Deepening (Hours 8–20)
The game's central narrative: a series of Lelembut situations that the community addresses together, each one revealing more about the world's history, the community's past, and Sari's own history. Each situation is a contained story with emotional completeness — the game can be played as a collection of narrative episodes rather than a single linear arc.

The deepening section is also when the game's secondary characters develop into full people — when the player understands that everyone in the Rumah Ragam has the same question Sari has, in their own way, at their own stage of trying to answer it.

### The Crisis (Hours 20–28)
A situation that requires the full community to act together — and forces a confrontation with the Pemimpin Roh. The crisis is not an action sequence. It is a philosophical confrontation: what does it mean to have power over something you don't fully understand?

### Resolution (Hours 28–35)
Not a triumphant ending. The resolution of the main conflict changes the community — some things are gained, some things are lost. Sari doesn't have the answer to her question. She has better company in living with it.

The final scene is Sari and one other character (chosen by player relationship choices throughout the game) sitting at the edge of the Pulau Tengah, watching the sea. No dramatic revelation. No earned wisdom delivered in dialogue. Just two people, present, with the same unreducible experience between them.

## VISUAL STYLE DIRECTION

### The Core Visual Decision
**Semi-realistic with specific painterly qualities.** This is not photorealistic — the world should feel like it could be painted, could be dreamed, could be remembered rather than photographed. But it is not so stylized that it loses the specific textures of Indonesian reality: the particular shade of red-brown of Java's laterite soil, the specific quality of tropical afternoon light filtering through bamboo, the density and noise of a Southeast Asian urban street.

**Reference visual sensibility:**
- The environmental painting quality of *Ori and the Blind Forest* at a more realistic register
- The specific Indonesian textural reality of contemporary Indonesian illustrators like Diela Maharanie or Khairul Asyraf (not their specific style, but their attention to cultural specificity in visual detail)
- The layered, ambient quality of *Night in the Woods*' environments — the sense of a world that exists beyond the frame

**What it must NOT look like:**
- Generic Southeast Asian aesthetics — batik patterns as decorative elements, Balinese dance imagery, tourist-poster tropicality
- JRPG visual conventions — this is not a Japanese aesthetic, even though JRPGs are the game's closest genre relatives
- Western fantasy conventions filtered through an Indonesian setting — the visual design must come from Indonesian visual traditions directly

---

### Spirit World Visual Treatment
When *Rasa* perception is active — when the membrane is perceptible — the visual layer shifts:

- Color temperature shifts slightly warmer — not dramatically, not like a filter, but the way a room's light changes when candles are lit
- Specific visual frequencies: a kind of photorealistic double-exposure, where the spirit-world layer is overlaid at approximately 40% opacity on the physical world
- The spirit-world layer has the specific visual quality of *batik tulis* — not the pattern, but the quality of ink on fabric: slightly blurred edges, organic variation, the evidence of a human hand
- Sound design changes simultaneously (see Audio Direction)

## CHARACTER DIRECTION

### SARI — The Protagonist
**Age:** 24
**Background:** Born in a small town in Nusa Barat. Youngest of four siblings. Her *Rasa* manifested at 12. She managed it alone for 12 years — not in crisis, but in the specific quiet management of someone who has learned to live with something others don't share.

**Visual direction:** She looks specific — not generic "Indonesian protagonist." Her style is the specific style of a young Indonesian woman navigating between traditional and contemporary culture with her own particular choices: not uniform, not performed, specific to her history. She wears the same few things in different combinations — not because of budget limitations (she is in a city now, with a job) but because she is not someone who performs identity through clothing.

**Personality in gameplay:** Sari is perceptive (players who pay attention will notice she observes more than she speaks), careful (she does not make decisions before she understands what she's deciding), and unexpectedly funny in the specific dry way of people who have spent a lot of time watching from the edges. She is not a hero in the conventional sense — she is a person who does what she understands is right, in contexts where "right" is rarely obvious.

---

### PAK WIRYO — Elder of the Rumah Ragam
**Age:** 71
**Background:** A retired civil engineer who spent 40 years building bridges while also managing a *Rasa* that made him, as he describes it, "aware of everything the bridge would eventually become, while he was building it." He is pragmatic, specific, and deeply wise in a way that presents as ordinary conversation about practical things.

**Visual direction:** Looks exactly like a retired Indonesian civil engineer — which is to say, like a person with a life's worth of specific experience visible in his face and body. He dresses practically. He carries a small notebook. He has reading glasses that he is always slightly surprised to need.

**Role in the narrative:** The mentor figure, but not the ideal wise mentor — a human mentor who has his own unresolved questions, his own preferences that sometimes cloud his judgment, his own grief about things the game reveals slowly.

---

### DEWI — Contemporary practitioner, 29
**Background:** University-educated, works in cultural documentation (she records endangered music traditions). Her *Rasa* is specific to sound — she perceives the spirit world primarily through acoustic experience. She hears what others don't.

**Visual direction:** Urban, precise. The specific style of a Jakarta creative professional who cares about Indonesian contemporary design — she wears local labels, she is aesthetically sophisticated without being performative about it.

**Role in the narrative:** Sari's peer and eventual closest companion. The relationship develops slowly, without forced milestones — it deepens because they spend time doing things together that matter.

## AUDIO DIRECTION

### Musical Identity
**Primary instrument:** The gamelan — but not as decoration and not as cultural shorthand. The game's musical identity is built on exploring what gamelan can be in a contemporary, emotionally complex context.

Specific approach: a collaboration with contemporary Indonesian composers who work at the intersection of gamelan and contemporary composition (composers working in the tradition of Rahayu Supanggah or Alus). The score should feel like it couldn't be from anywhere else — not because it uses gamelan as an ethnic marker, but because it uses the specific logic of gamelan music (cyclical structure, interlocking patterns, the gong cycle as temporal anchor) as the basis for the game's musical language.

**Silence as a compositional choice:** Many of the game's most important moments will have no music — only ambient sound. The game's musical system must distinguish between:
1. Silence as rest (between events)
2. Silence as tension (the moment before something important)
3. Silence as emotional weight (the aftermath of something significant)

### Sound Design Identity
The game's ambient sound is built from field recordings of specific Indonesian locations — specific neighborhoods in Yogyakarta, specific beaches in Flores, specific markets in Jakarta. The sound of this world is the actual sound of Indonesia, organized and elevated but not homogenized.

The spirit-world ambient layer: when *Rasa* perception is active, the ambient sound shifts — not dramatically, but specifically. The specific acoustic change is: the reduction of human-made sound and the increase in natural sound. The spirit world is older than the human world, and its acoustic character reflects that.

## NARRATIVE STRUCTURE

### Core Structure: Episodic Anthology within a continuous arc
The game is structured as a series of self-contained Lelembut stories (10–12 stories, each 2–4 hours) nested within a continuous narrative about Sari's integration into the Rumah Ragam community.

This structure serves both the game's creative vision and its production reality: episodic stories can be developed and tested independently, ensuring each chapter is emotionally complete, while the continuous narrative provides the reason to keep playing.

### Player Choice Philosophy
RAGAM JIWA does not have "good" and "bad" endings. It has choices whose consequences are consistent with the world's internal logic — where sometimes the choice that seems compassionate has difficult consequences, and sometimes the choice that seems pragmatic is the right one for reasons only clarity in hindsight reveals.

Choice in RAGAM JIWA is not moral optimization. It is the act of being a specific person — Sari — in specific situations, without perfect information, without the ability to replay life. The player can make different choices on replay, but the first playthrough should feel like living a life, not solving a puzzle.
